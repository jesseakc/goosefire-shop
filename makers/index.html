The artist

Alesha

Alesha is the artist behind Goose Fire. She builds the forms, chooses what to make, and decides how each piece should look. Everything in this studio comes out of her judgement.

A studio she assembled

Alesha built this studio the way most people build one — one find at a time. The kiln came from a Facebook Marketplace listing for a hundred dollars. The pottery wheel came the same way, for not much more than that. The rest turned up the same way, alongside the shelving, the benches, and the second-hand tools that came with them.

That is not a story about getting by. It is a story about initiative — a person deciding what she needed to make work, and going and getting it.

Where the creativity enters

Moulds she makes herself

The moulds our ornaments are cast from are Alesha's work. The forms are not: they come from 3D files by Old Forge Creations, who design and print the plastic forms that plaster is poured into.

She casts the plaster, produces the moulds, and does everything after that. It took more than two months of evenings to cast enough to begin production.

No two are the same

The moulds we use are common and popular, and you can buy the same files we did. What makes an ornament ours is everything that happens after the cast: how the piece is prepared, how it is trimmed and cleaned, and above all how it is glazed. Two ornaments from the same mould on the same afternoon will not come out of the kiln alike.

Each stage is slow and hands-on — casting the plaster, pouring the slip, easing the piece out, trimming it, cleaning it, glazing it — and each one leaves its own mark on the result. It is a lot of time per ornament, and it is the reason no two are identical.

Play is part of the work

Give Alesha a goose and she will give it a personality. The pigs are the clearest example — cast forms she then modifies until they stop being quite what the mould intended, given expressions and details nobody specified. Geese, pumpkins, and whatever else the moulds turn up get the same treatment.

Some pieces are wheel-thrown, some are slipcast from moulds, and some are carved or assembled from cast parts. What they share is the decision-making.

What it takes

Alesha holds a full-time office job, and she does this as well. The photographs in this studio were taken across late evenings — the timestamps on this library run past eleven at night, more than once. She gives up sleep for it. That is not a hardship to be pitied; it is just what building a practice out of spare hours actually costs.

Her aim is to make this her full-time work. It is not there yet, and nothing on this site pretends otherwise.
